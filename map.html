How these sites were found — from orbit

Nobody surveyed these on the ground. Every site here was spotted by satellite, and the size figures are derived from what the sensor actually measured.

  1. The satellite sees the fire. NASA’s VIIRS instrument passes overhead each night reading infrared. A gas flare burns at roughly 1,500–1,900 K, far hotter than anything else on the landscape, so it stands out unmistakably against cold ground.
  2. Location comes from the pixel. Each detection is fixed to about 375 m — the sensor’s resolution. That is the coordinate you see: accurate to about a wellpad, not to a particular stack.
  3. Size comes from the heat. Temperature and the glowing area together give radiant heat output, which converts to gas volume in billions of cubic metres per year using a calibration fitted against reported production figures.
  4. Gas becomes power. Volume converts to megawatts through two constants: pipeline gas carries about 1,000 BTU per cubic foot, and a reciprocating generator burns roughly 10,000 BTU per kWh. That works out to 1 MMcf/day ≈ 4 MW — so a site burning 250 Mcf/day supports about 1 MW, or roughly 285 miners.
  5. Six years, cross-checked. Annual surveys from 2017 to 2022 are matched against each other: detections within 500 m in different years are treated as the same site. How many years a site appears in is its persistence — the strongest signal that it is a real ongoing operation rather than a short well test.

Reading the globe: column height is power potential on a log scale, so an 11 MW site towers over a 150 kW one. Colour is persistence — green means it burned in every one of the six survey years. Markers shrink as you zoom in, so dense basins pull apart into individual sites instead of staying a blob.

Two different satellites, two different jobs. The size figures above come from the annual EOG survey, which ends in 2022 — that is where the open data stops, so every MW number is dated. A second free NASA feed (FIRMS) updates every few hours and is used only to answer whether a flare is still burning. It cannot measure volume, so it never changes a site’s size.

Three VIIRS satellites are queried (SNPP, NOAA-20, NOAA-21), which between them pass overhead several times a night. Because FIRMS reports every fire on earth, a detection only counts when it recurs at the same point on three or more separate nights and lands on a flare the survey already knew about. A wildfire moves and will not do both. Where a site shows no confirmation that means the satellite did not see it — cloud, a flare below the detection floor, or genuinely dark. Those are different things, so the panel says not confirmed rather than not burning.

What the satellite cannot tell you: the exact wellhead, gas composition, H₂S content, road or winter access, or whether anyone will sell it to you. Volumes are dated 2022 — a site burning through 2022 is very likely still burning, but this cannot confirm today. Those gaps stay blank on each site rather than being guessed.

Scenario

Scenario — the numbers everything is priced against

Every figure on this page — cash cost, payback, break-even, the portfolio totals — is calculated from these five inputs. They start at today’s real market.

Change one and the whole page re-prices instantly. Drop BTC to $40,000 and every site recalculates, so you can see which ones still work and which stop working. Switch Colour sites by to Margin and the map turns red as sites fall below their cash cost.

Three of them are your assumptions, not quotes:
Power rate — what you expect to pay the producer per kWh.
Build cost — your all-in buildout per kW of capacity.
Take-or-pay — the share of capacity you must pay for whether you use it or not.

Nothing here is saved onto a site or sent anywhere — it is a what-if. Reset to live puts the real market numbers back, and the bar always states whether you are looking at live or hypothetical values.

Portfolio

Portfolio — what if you built all of these?

Tick sites in the list and this treats them as a single operation: their combined hashrate, monthly bitcoin, total capital, and blended cost to mine one coin.

The big number at the top is the one that matters most. The combined take-or-pay floor is what you owe every month even with every miner switched off. Revenue stops in a downturn; that bill does not. It is the number that ends operations, and nothing else in this app surfaces it.

Portfolio break-even is the BTC price at which the whole selection stops covering its power bill. Above it you are making money; below it every site in the group is losing.

All of it re-prices live from the Scenario bar above — so you can select five sites, drag BTC down, and watch exactly where the group stops working. None of these sites has been quoted, so the commercial terms are your assumptions.

Tick sites in the list to model them together. The combined take-or-pay floor is the monthly obligation you owe whether or not a single miner runs.
